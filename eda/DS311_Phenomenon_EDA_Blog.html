The Philosophy of Looking: How Topic 01 Cured My Dataset-Hopping Panic

Before entering DS311, I treated data science as a race to fit algorithms. Whenever a dataset resisted, I abandoned it for something easier. Then Ma'am Chris introduced Francis Anscombe and John Tukey — proving that numbers alone can lie, and why looking at messy anomalies is where the real science begins.

Rogelio Q. Mandamian III DS3A • Exploratory Data Analysis • USTP
Instructor: Ma'am Chris Jallaine S. Mugot 7 min read Oct 2026

My Backward "Confirmatory" Rush

Let me start with an honest confession: before this semester, I was approaching data science completely backward.

Whenever our instructors handed us an analytical assignment, my immediate instinct was to formulate a conclusion in my head first, download a dataset, and rush to ask: “Does this confirm what I want to prove?” I skipped the foundational groundwork entirely. I would jump directly into multi-variable scatter plots, high-dimensional projections, and correlation heatmaps without spending even five minutes understanding what the individual columns actually contained.

I didn't pay attention to whether a feature had a severe right skew, whether its variance was dominated by two or three massive outliers, or what the units of measurement physically represented. To me, a dataset was simply a monolithic block of numbers that I needed to shove into an algorithm as quickly as possible.

Inevitably, reality pushed back hard. I would encounter columns with 30% null values. A variable would contain negative numbers where only positive readings made physical sense. Or a correlation coefficient would read as a weak r = 0.12 when I had convinced myself a strong relationship existed.

And whenever I hit those roadblocks, I panicked. I had memorized textbook terms like “mean imputation” and “median imputation,” but in practice, I had little intuition for what damage naive choices caused. I treated missingness like an annoying bug to be erased — blindly writing df.fillna(df.mean()) just to stop my code from crashing. When the resulting models collapsed or made nonsense predictions, I fell into chronic dataset-hopping: abandoning the file at 1:30 AM to hunt Kaggle for something “cleaner.”

“It is very common to spend the most time in the exploratory data analysis stage in order to have a well-made output and to prevent future inconvenience.”
— Ma'am Chris Jallaine S. Mugot, DS311 Instructor

That single quote from Ma'am Chris during our opening lectures completely reframed my perspective. EDA isn't a tedious pre-processing chore you rush through to reach machine learning; it is the fundamental sensory phase where you earn the right to model.

One Example I Recall: Stumbling Upon the Egypt Dataset

To give a concrete example — or perhaps the easiest one I can recall from my experience — back in our 2nd-year DATAVIS course, my group and I were caught right in the middle of this dataset-hopping habit. We had spent hours scouting Kaggle, loading candidate files into Google Colab, hitting bizarre percentages and corrupted records, and abandoning them one by one.

We hopped through multiple dead ends until we finally stumbled upon the Egypt Population Density dataset.

The Egypt dataset wasn't really a problem — it was simply the one clean, structured dataset that felt safe enough to work with at the time. It contained reliable records of Egypt's governorates, total census counts, and inhabited land areas. Stumbling upon it felt like striking gold because it gave us a dependable empirical foundation to map the dramatic demographic contrast between the hyper-dense Nile Valley corridor and the vast, sparsely inhabited desert governorates.

Yet even with clean data, our initial raw plots failed completely. Because Cairo and Giza had population densities exceeding tens of thousands of people per square kilometer, plotting the distribution on a standard linear scale flattened every other governorate against the baseline. Matani and the New Valley appeared as empty zeroes.

Without realizing it at the time, we had stumbled upon John Tukey's core doctrine: Re-expression. To make the geographic reality visible, we had to transform the scale using $\log_{10}(\text{density})$. The moment we applied that logarithmic lens, the heavy right tail decompressed, and the real demographic structure of Egypt emerged.

Act II • The Empirical Proof

When Numbers Mislead: Francis Anscombe's 1973 Wake-Up Call

In our first week of DS311, Ma'am Chris introduced us to a demonstration that permanently dismantled my trust in uninspected numbers: Anscombe's Quartet.

Constructed in 1973 by British statistician Francis Anscombe, the quartet consists of four synthetic datasets, each containing eleven $(x, y)$ pairs. When you run standard descriptive statistics across all four, the numbers agree with terrifying precision:

Statistical Metric Dataset I Dataset II Dataset III Dataset IV
Mean of X (μx) 9.00 9.00 9.00 9.00
Mean of Y (μy) 7.50 7.50 7.50 7.50
Variance of X (σ²x) 11.00 11.00 11.00 11.00
Variance of Y (σ²y) 4.125 4.125 4.125 4.125
Pearson Correlation (r) 0.816 0.816 0.816 0.816
Linear Regression Fit y = 3.00 + 0.50x y = 3.00 + 0.50x y = 3.00 + 0.50x y = 3.00 + 0.50x
Coefficient of Det. (R²) 0.67 0.67 0.67 0.67

If you judged these four datasets purely through an API call or a Jupyter summary table — df.describe() — you would conclude that all four represent identical linear physical phenomena.

Yet the moment you plot them on a scatter canvas, the illusion shatters:

✦ What Looking Reveals

Dataset I is a well-behaved linear trend with moderate gaussian scatter. Dataset II is a deterministic quadratic curve with zero linear validity. Dataset III is a tight linear association thrown off by a single vertical outlier. And Dataset IV is a vertical cluster where a single extreme leverage point at $x = 19$ artificially manufactures an r = 0.816 correlation out of thin air.

“Numerical quantities focus on expected values; visual displays force us to notice what we never anticipated.”
— Francis J. Anscombe, 1973

This was the wake-up call I needed. Calculating summary statistics without visualizing the data is like buying a house from an aerial blueprint without ever inspecting the foundation.

The Core Antidote: John Tukey's Four R's

In 1977, John Tukey published his landmark work, Exploratory Data Analysis. He offered a principled philosophy to rescue researchers from the trap of blind metric-fitting. He organized this mindset around four foundational pillars: The Four R's.

R1

Resistance

Classical statistics like the arithmetic mean have a breakdown point of 0% — a single extreme outlier at infinity pulls the entire mean to infinity. Tukey championed resistant measures like the median and interquartile range (IQR), which have a 50% breakdown point. Even if half your sample consists of corrupted readings, the median remains anchored to where real life lives.

R2

Residuals

Tukey stated that $\text{Data} = \text{Fit} + \text{Residual}$. Never be satisfied with a model just because $R^2$ looks respectable. The residual — what the model failed to capture — is where genuine discoveries reside. Inspecting residual plots exposes non-linear curvature, heteroscedastic fan shapes, and sensor dropouts that summary metrics hide.

R3

Re-expression

Raw measurements rarely arrive on the optimal mathematical scale. Applying monotonic transformations like $\log_{10}(x)$, $\sqrt{x}$, or $1/x$ straightens curved associations, stabilizes non-constant variance, and compresses heavy right tails — exactly as we experienced when decompressing Egypt's municipal population densities.

R4

Revelation

Visual displays allow the analyst to perceive what they never anticipated. As Tukey famously wrote: “The greatest value of a picture is when it forces us to notice what we never expected to see.” A well-crafted visualization is a cognitive instrument that turns passive numbers into active revelation.

A Field Guide: How I Walk Through the 7 Steps

In traditional coursework, we were conditioned to follow rigid, linear checklists: plug in numbers, execute code, deliver a clean report. But as Ma'am Chris emphasized in Topic 01, exploratory analysis is an organic, iterative cycle of inquiry. Here is how I approach a new dataset today:

01

Understand Context & Origin

Data never exists in a vacuum. Before writing any code, I investigate who collected the records, under what protocols, and for what purpose. In our CDO Health Access capstone, knowing whether clinic coordinates came from volunteer OpenStreetMap tags or verified Department of Health audits determined the entire credibility of our spatial buffers.

✦ Grounding in Reality
02

Inspect Data Structure

My mandatory first reflex in Jupyter is running df.shape and df.info(). I verify column data types, check for accidental string encodings on numeric values, and audit row duplicate hashes. Catching structural defects early prevents mysterious syntax errors later.

✦ Turning on the Lights
03

Assess True Data Quality

Missing values are not random errors to wipe with fillna(mean). In our Lab 01 Water Potability dataset, missing chemical readings reflected laboratory equipment limitations at rural monitoring stations. Understanding missingness mechanisms (MCAR vs. MAR vs. MNAR) informs whether you impute, model missingness explicitly, or retain it as an informative signal.

✦ Informative Missingness
04

Examine Univariate Distributions

Audit every variable individually before pairing them. Compare the arithmetic mean with the median: a large gap immediately signals skewness or outlier leverage. Inspect histograms across multiple bin counts to avoid underbinned flattening, and identify heavy tails early.

✦ Spotting Heavy Tails
05

Examine Bivariate Relationships

Scatter plots and grouped box plots expose relationships that correlation coefficients conceal. In NYC Taxi data, plotting distance against fare amount revealed fixed flat-rate airport trips where 0 miles cost $52, and sensor glitches where 120 miles cost $8 — real-world phenomena no single r value could summarize.

✦ Lenses Over Locks
06

Identify Anomalies as Stories

Instead of immediately purging outliers, ask: “Does this observation make physical sense?” An extreme measurement might be a data logging error, or it might be the most valuable, informative signal in the entire sample. The anomaly is where domain understanding happens.

✦ The Genuine Story
07

Iterate & Refine Hypotheses

Exploratory analysis never terminates in a static slide deck. Every finding raises sharper questions. When an unexpected pattern surfaces, loop back: re-express scales, partition subgroups, and challenge assumptions before committing to predictive modeling.

✦ The Detective's Mindset

Epilogue & Course Repositories
