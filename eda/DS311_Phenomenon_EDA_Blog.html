Reflective Confession

My Backward "Confirmatory" Rush

Let me share an honest confession about how I used to approach data science before entering DS311: I was doing almost everything completely backward.

Whenever our professors gave us an analytical assignment, my immediate instinct was to formulate a conclusion in my head first, load the dataset, and rush to ask: “Does this confirm what I want to prove?” I would skip the foundational legwork and jump straight into multivariate scatter plots, high-dimensional projections, and correlation heatmaps without spending even five minutes inspecting what the individual columns actually contained.

I didn't pay attention to whether a feature had a severe right skew, whether the variance was dominated by a couple of massive outliers, or what the units of measurement even meant. To me, a dataset was just a monolithic block of numbers that I wanted to shove into an algorithm as fast as possible.

Inevitably, reality would push back hard. I would find a column with 30% null values, negative readings where only positive numbers made physical sense, or a weak correlation of r = 0.12 when I was convinced a massive relationship existed.

And whenever I hit those roadblocks, I panicked. I treated missing values like an annoying bug to be erased — blindly writing df.fillna(df.mean()) just to stop my code from crashing. When the resulting models fell apart, I fell into chronic dataset-hopping.

“It is very common to spend the most time in the exploratory data analysis stage in order to have a well-made output and to prevent future inconvenience.”
— Ma'am Chris Jallaine S. Mugot, DS311 Instructor

Tukey's Core Philosophy • 1977

The Four R's of Exploratory Data Analysis

Instead of asking, “Does this confirm my hypothesis?”, John Tukey taught us that EDA asks, “What is this data trying to tell me?” These Four R's ✦ Tukey's 1977 Doctrine Residuals (what models miss), Re-expression (scale transformations), Resistance (insensitivity to outliers), and Revelation (visual discovery). are my personal data science compass:

R1
Resistance
Rawls' Veil of Ignorance • Median Over Mean
Philosopher John Rawls asked: behind a veil of ignorance, how would you judge society? If four villagers have 20 coins and a king has 5,000, the arithmetic mean ($1,016$) claims the village is wealthy — a total lie. Tukey's median has a 50% breakdown point, remaining firmly at $20$.
Philosophical Core: Summaries must protect the ordinary reality, not be hijacked by single extreme monarchs.
R2
Residuals
The Black Swan Paradox • Data = Fit + Residual
A thousand white swans cannot confirm the law that all swans are white, but a single black swan residual ($y_i - \hat{y}_i$) breaks the illusion. In modeling, we never settle for what fits; we inspect the residuals to discover the non-linear curvature that straight regression lines missed entirely.
Philosophical Core: Residuals are messages, not errors. What the pattern missed is where genuine truth lives.
R3
Re-expression
Pascal's Two Infinities • Transform to Clarify
Blaise Pascal noted humans hang between two infinities — the galactic and atomic. Plot them linearly, and everything except galaxies vanishes! In our Egypt data, Cairo squashed the rest of the country flat. Taking log10(density) scaled orders of magnitude so both Nile cities and desert governorates became visible.
Philosophical Core: Transformations aren't cheating; they are the analytical microscope of multi-scale reality.
R4
Revelation
Plato's Allegory of the Cave • Look Directly
In Plato's cave, prisoners spend lifetimes studying and averaging shadows on the wall. Summary tables are the shadows; revelation is turning around to look at the physical geometry directly. A single scatter plot shatters identical correlations in seconds.
Philosophical Core: If your analysis hasn't forced you to notice what you never expected, you haven't finished looking.

Practitioner Framework • Topic 01

A Cycle of Questions: How I Actually Walk Through the 7 Steps

In traditional mathematics, I was conditioned to follow rigid, step-by-step formulas: plug in numbers, calculate the result, get the single right answer. In Topic 01, Ma'am Chris taught us that EDA is not governed by a universal checklist — it is an iterative cycle shaped by the data itself:

01

Understand Context and the Data Source

In earlier DATAVIS projects, I used to ignore where data came from entirely — I would just download a CSV from Kaggle and immediately start typing code. When working on our CDO Health Access project, I learned that knowing whether healthcare clinic coordinates came from OpenStreetMap volunteers versus official DOH or CBMS censuses determined what the records truly meant before writing a single line of code. Data never exists in a vacuum — knowing collection protocols precedes analysis.

✦ Grounding in Origin
02

Inspect the Structure of the Data

My old bad habit was jumping straight into plotting charts without checking the data's skeleton first. I would run into bizarre syntax crashes halfway through simply because a numeric column was formatted as an object string, or because the dataset had thousands of hidden duplicate rows. Now, the moment I load any dataframe into Jupyter, my mandatory first reflex is running df.shape, df.info(), and auditing non-null counts before any charting.

✦ Turning on the Lights
03

Assess Data Quality

This was my biggest wake-up call during our Lab 01 Water Potability activity and our Diabetes 130-US Hospitals dataset. In 2nd year, I treated missing values like a nuisance to be wiped with fillna(mean). I learned that missingness is often selective and informative, not random. But when auditing 3,276 water samples, I discovered that missing Sulfate and Trihalomethanes readings were selective laboratory omissions because rural stations lacked chromatography equipment. And in the diabetes data, weight was missing in 97% of patient records!

✦ Selective Missingness
04

Examine Individual Variables (Univariate & Binning)

Before Ma'am Chris taught us Topic 01, I completely ignored single variables — I rushed for multi-variable interactions right away. But looking at raw distributions taught me that comparing median against mean is the fastest test for skewness. Furthermore, histograms are sensitive to bin counts — too few bins conceals real bimodality, while too many overfits to noise!

# Tukey Resistant Fences & Log10 Re-expression
import numpy as np

# 1. Tame heavy right tails (e.g. Egypt population density)
df['log_density'] = np.log10(df['pop_density'] + 1)

# 2. Compute Tukey Resistant Fences (1.5 x IQR)
q1, q3 = df['log_density'].quantile([0.25, 0.75])
iqr = q3 - q1
lower_fence = q1 - 1.5 * iqr
upper_fence = q3 + 1.5 * iqr
✦ Spotting Heavy Tails & Binning Artifacts
05

Examine Relationships Between Variables (Bivariate & Lenses)

When I reached Lab 04 (NYC Yellow Taxi Trips) and paired trip distance with fare amount, bivariate inspection became real. A single scalar like Pearson's r would have tricked me into believing a linear trend existed, but bivariate scatter plots revealed non-linear rate ceilings and sensor dropouts where 0 miles cost $50, and GPS glitches where 150 miles cost $10! In our CDO Health Access research, examining travel times against vulnerability revealed spatial divides no single variable could show.

✦ Lenses, Not Locks
06

Identify Patterns, Anomalies, and Surprises

This is the core orientation Ma'am Chris instilled in me. Instead of passively writing code, I constantly ask: “Does this number make physical sense in the real world? If not, why?” When I saw 0-mile taxi trips with $50 fares in Lab 04, or rural barangays with zero primary health facilities in our CDO spatial study, those weren't errors to throw away — they were the real story!

✦ The Genuine Story
07

Formulate Questions and Hypotheses

I used to think that the goal of data analysis was to finish with an undisputed, final answer on day one. But as Ma'am Chris reminded us, EDA doesn't give you final answers — it equips you with sharper, testable hypotheses grounded in reality. When I finish exploring a dataset now, I walk away with informed, testable hypotheses: “Why do upland barangays show acute travel-time impedance?” or “Does patient readmission spike when HbA1c isn't tested during inpatient admission?”

✦ Curiosity into Science
Oral Examination • Diagnostic Defense Probes

Frequently Anticipated Defense Inquiries

During research and laboratory defense panels, faculty scrutinize how deeply you understand data structure before algorithms touch it. Here is how I frame my diagnostic answers grounded in Topic 01 fundamentals:

01
Why can't an analyst jump straight into training an XGBoost or deep learning model?
+
Because algorithms minimize mathematical loss functions over numbers, regardless of whether those numbers reflect physical truth or corrupted data artifacts. If a feature contains undetected outliers, extreme leverage points, or heavy right-skew, gradient trees and neural networks will overfit to the artifacts. Exploratory Data Analysis is the sole phase that verifies empirical validity before computational execution.
02
Why did Francis Anscombe construct four datasets with the exact same correlation (r ≈ 0.816)?
+
To surgically dismantle the illusion that summary statistics tell the whole story. Without plotting, Dataset I (standard linear), Dataset II (non-linear quadratic parabola), Dataset III (clean line + single vertical outlier), and Dataset IV (vertical cluster + single high-leverage point) share the exact same mean (μx = 9.0), variance, regression slope (β1 ≈ 0.50), and R² (66.6%). Looking is non-negotiable because summary statistics are blind to geometry.
03
How does Tukey's concept of Resistance protect against real-world data corruption?
+
Classical parametric metrics like the sample mean have a breakdown point of 0% — a single corrupted recording at infinity pulls the entire mean to infinity. In contrast, John Tukey's resistant measures (medians, IQR, Tukey fences) have a 50% breakdown point. Even if half your sample consists of wild logging spikes, resistant summaries preserve the true underlying central tendency without premature data deletion.
04
How did the Egypt Population Density dataset break the habit of dataset-hopping?
+
In past projects, discovering a heavily skewed distribution made me panic and discard the data. With the Egypt dataset, we embraced Tukey's second R: Re-expression. By transforming the raw population density into a log10 scale, we tamed the extreme orders of magnitude between Cairo and the frontier desert governorates — transforming an unreadable clump into an interpretable, publication-grade cartographic artifact.
